# Concepts

Plain-language explanations of the trading ideas a beginner meets in this app. Retrieved verbatim
by Axl's `explain_concept` tool — the model does not paraphrase these, so the wording here is the
wording the user reads. Edit freely; the file hot-reloads.

Every entry follows the same four beats, and the third and fourth are what make it teaching
rather than a dictionary:

1. what it is, in one plain sentence
2. why it exists — the job it does
3. what it costs you — the honest trade-off
4. the mistake beginners actually make

DESCRIBE, NEVER PRESCRIBE. Explain what a thing is and how it behaves; never tell the reader what
their number should be. Where a convention is worth knowing, name it as a convention ("many
traders risk 1–2% per trade"), never as an instruction. Deciding *this* user's stop or size is a
desk's job, and the difference between the two is the whole reason Axl is allowed to teach.

---

## stop
Aliases: stop loss, stop-loss, stoploss, stops, sl, stop order

A stop is a price you pick in advance where you'll get out if the trade goes against you.

It exists to make the size of your loss a decision instead of an outcome. Without one, the answer
to "how much can this cost me?" is "however much it moves before I can't watch any more" — and
that is usually decided at the worst possible moment, by whichever feeling is loudest. Setting it
beforehand, while you're calm and nothing is at stake, is the entire point.

What it costs you: real trades get stopped out and then go the way you expected. That is not a
malfunction, it is the price of the protection — you are paying in occasional missed winners for a
cap on the losers. A stop placed too close pays that price constantly; one placed too far barely
caps anything.

The beginner mistake is moving it. Price approaches the stop, the loss becomes real, and it feels
obvious to give the trade "a bit more room". Doing that converts a known, survivable loss into an
unknown one, and it's how small mistakes become account-sized ones.

## take-profit
Aliases: take profit, tp, target, price target, profit target

A take-profit is a price you pick in advance where you'll close a winning trade and bank it.

It exists for the same reason a stop does — to make the decision before you're in the middle of
it. A position going your way is genuinely hard to close, because every gain suggests there might
be more.

What it costs you: you will often watch price keep running after you're out. You are trading the
top of the move for the certainty of actually collecting something, and that is a real trade, not
a free one.

The beginner mistake is the mirror of the stop mistake — moving the target further away each time
price approaches it, so a winner is never actually taken, and eventually it isn't a winner.

## entry-zone
Aliases: entry, entry zone, entry zones, zone, the zone, entry range

An entry zone is a price *range* where a trade becomes worth taking, rather than a single exact
price.

It exists because markets don't respect exact numbers. A level you care about is really an area —
somewhere buyers showed up before, somewhere a move stalled — and demanding one precise price to
the cent means missing trades that were right in every way that mattered.

What it costs you: your actual entry can land anywhere in the zone, so your real risk and your
real reward are ranges too, not the tidy numbers on the plan.

The beginner mistake is chasing. Price runs past the zone, the trade looks like it's working, and
buying in anyway feels like catching up. But entering above the zone means a wider stop for a
smaller remaining move — the same idea with worse arithmetic.

## risk
Aliases: risk, risking, risk per trade, at risk

Risk is the money you'd lose if a trade goes to your stop — decided up front, before you enter.

It exists as a *number* so that "am I comfortable with this?" has an answer you can check rather
than a feeling. "Risking 2%" means that if this trade fails completely, your account is 2%
smaller. It says nothing about how much you might make.

What it costs you: keeping risk small keeps individual wins small too. There is no setting where
losses are capped and gains are not, and any strategy that seems to offer one is hiding the risk
somewhere you haven't looked yet.

The beginner mistake is thinking about risk per trade and never about risk in total. Six positions
each risking 2% are not six separate 2% risks if they all move together — in a bad week they can
behave like one 12% risk.

## position-size
Aliases: position size, size, sizing, position sizing, quantity, how much to buy

Position size is how much of a thing you buy — and it is what actually converts your stop into
your risk.

It exists as the dial you control. You can't choose how far price moves, but you choose how much
you own while it does. The same stop with twice the size is twice the loss, so size is where the
risk number is really set.

What it costs you: sizing to keep the loss tolerable usually means a smaller position than feels
worth bothering with, especially on a wide stop. That feeling is the constraint doing its job.

The beginner mistake is deciding size by what feels affordable to buy, rather than by what the
loss would be if the stop is hit. Many traders work backwards instead — from the loss they're
willing to take and the distance to the stop — and a common convention is to keep that loss to
1–2% of the account per trade.

## drawdown
Aliases: drawdown, dd, max drawdown, drawdowns

A drawdown is how far your account has fallen from its highest point.

It exists because the ending balance hides the journey. Two accounts can finish the year at the
same number, having felt nothing alike — one drifting gently, the other down 40% in March.
Drawdown is the measure of what you actually had to sit through.

What it costs you: losses need bigger gains to undo. Down 20% needs 25% to get back; down 50%
needs 100%. Deep holes get disproportionately harder to climb out of, which is why capping the
depth matters more than chasing the height.

The beginner mistake is judging a strategy by its best stretch and assuming the worst stretch is
survivable. The number that ends most accounts isn't the return — it's the drawdown that arrived
before the return did.

## r-multiple
Aliases: r, r multiple, rmultiple, 1r, 2r, r-multiples

R is the size of your risk on a trade, used as a unit of measurement. If you risk £100 and make
£250, you made 2.5R.

It exists so results can be compared. A £300 win on a huge position and a £300 win on a tiny one
are not the same achievement, and raw money hides that. Counting in R strips out size and leaves
the part that reflects the decision.

What it costs you: R says nothing about how often you're right. A strategy averaging +2R can still
lose money if it only works one time in five, so the multiple has to be read next to the hit rate,
never alone.

The beginner mistake is treating a high R as proof a strategy is good. Big R usually comes with a
low win rate and long losing stretches, and most people abandon the approach during one of them —
which converts a winning method into a losing experience.

## risk-reward
Aliases: risk reward, risk/reward, rr, r:r, reward to risk, risk to reward

Risk-reward compares what you stand to lose against what you stand to make, before you enter. A
2:1 trade aims to make twice what it risks.

It exists as a sanity check. It costs nothing to work out before the trade and it answers a
question that's very hard to ask honestly once you're in one: is this worth doing at all?

What it costs you: it is an estimate, not a promise. The risk half is fairly reliable — you chose
the stop. The reward half is a guess about how far price will travel, and guesses drift toward
optimism.

The beginner mistake is producing the ratio you wanted by moving the target rather than by finding
a better trade. Any trade can be made to look 3:1 if the profit target is allowed to be
imaginative enough.

## long-and-short
Aliases: long, short, long and short, going long, going short, direction, bias

Going long means you own something and profit if the price rises. Going short means you profit if
it falls.

Both exist because a view can point either way. Being able to trade a fall means you don't have to
sit out markets you think are heading down.

What it costs you: the two are not mirror images. A long can lose at most what you put in, because
price stops at zero. A short has no such ceiling — there is no limit to how high a price can go,
so the loss has no natural floor, which is exactly why a stop matters more on a short, not less.

The beginner mistake is assuming shorting is just buying in reverse. It has different mechanics,
different costs to hold, and an asymmetry that only bites when you're wrong.

## market-vs-limit
Aliases: market order, limit order, market vs limit, order type, buy market, buy limit

A market order says "get me in now, whatever the price is". A limit order says "get me in only at
this price or better".

They exist for two different priorities. Market buys certainty of *getting in*; limit buys
certainty of *the price*. You cannot have both at once, and which one matters depends entirely on
the trade.

What it costs you: a market order can fill worse than the price you saw, especially when things
move fast or the market is thin — that gap is called slippage, and it comes out of your result. A
limit order protects you from that but may simply never fill, so you watch the trade happen
without you.

The beginner mistake is using market orders on everything because they always work. They always
work in the sense that they always fill — which is precisely what you don't want when price is
running away from you.

## fill
Aliases: fill, filled, fills, average price, average fill, execution price

A fill is the price you actually got, as opposed to the price you asked for.

The distinction exists because those two differ more often than people expect. Large orders may
fill in pieces at several prices, giving you an average rather than a single number.

What it costs you: your real profit and loss is measured from the fill, not from the plan. A trade
that looked like 2:1 on paper is something slightly worse in reality, every time, and those small
gaps compound across many trades.

The beginner mistake is recording the intended price rather than the actual one. Do that
consistently and your own trading record slowly becomes fiction — and it's the only record you
have to learn from.

## invalidation
Aliases: invalidation, invalidated, invalidation alert, thesis broken

Invalidation is the point where the reason you took the trade stops being true — regardless of
whether you're currently up or down.

It exists because a stop protects your money while invalidation protects your thinking. Price can
drift against you without breaking your reasoning, and it can also break your reasoning while
you're still in profit. Those are genuinely different events and they deserve different responses.

What it costs you: acting on invalidation means sometimes closing a trade that is winning, on the
grounds that it's winning for a reason you no longer believe. That is uncomfortable and it will
occasionally be wrong.

The beginner mistake is having no invalidation at all — only a stop. Then the only thing that can
ever tell you you're wrong is losing money, and by then it's the one form of feedback you've
already paid for.

## thesis
Aliases: thesis, the thesis, trade thesis, why

A thesis is the reason a trade exists, written down: what you think will happen, and why.

It exists so you have something to check the trade against later. A position with no stated reason
can't be reviewed — there is nothing to compare against what actually happened, so every outcome
just becomes luck.

What it costs you: writing it down makes being wrong explicit and legible, including to yourself.
That is unpleasant, and it is also the only thing that turns a losing trade into information.

The beginner mistake is a thesis so vague it can never be wrong — "it looks strong", "it's due a
bounce". If no future event could falsify it, it isn't a thesis, it's a mood.

## conviction
Aliases: conviction, confidence, conviction level

Conviction is how strongly you believe in *this particular* trade's reasoning, relative to your
other trades.

It exists to be comparable. It's a way of saying "this one is better founded than that one",
which is useful when deciding where attention and size should go.

What it costs you: conviction measures the quality of your reasoning, not the probability of being
right. High conviction trades lose all the time. Treating the two as the same is how a strong
opinion becomes an oversized position.

The beginner mistake is letting conviction rise with how much a trade has already moved. Feeling
more certain because you're up is the sensation of the position influencing the analysis, which is
backwards.

## paper-vs-live
Aliases: paper, paper trading, paper vs live, simulation, sim, demo, live trading

Paper trading places trades with real market prices but no real money. Live trading uses your
actual account.

Paper exists so you can learn the mechanics — how orders work, how a stop behaves, what a week of
your strategy actually looks like — without paying tuition in real losses.

What it costs you: it cannot simulate the part that is usually the problem. Paper losses don't
feel like anything, so the discipline that paper trading appears to prove is exactly the
discipline it never tested.

The beginner mistake is reading paper results as a forecast of live ones. The gap between the two
isn't in the strategy, it's in the person running it — and it tends to show up first in the
trades that hurt.

## why-a-stop-is-not-a-failure
Aliases: stopped out, why did i get stopped out, losing trade, taking a loss, stop hit, was i wrong

Getting stopped out means the plan worked. It is what a stop is for: the loss was capped at the
number you chose, when you chose it.

The distinction exists because it is easy to confuse a bad outcome with a bad decision. A good
trade can lose. A bad trade can win. Losses are a cost of participating, not evidence that
something has gone wrong.

What it costs you: accepting this means giving up the hope of a method that only wins, and taking
losses on schedule instead of hoping each one comes back. There is no version of trading without
losses, only versions where they're chosen and versions where they aren't.

The beginner mistake is treating each stop as a verdict and changing the whole approach after it.
Any method loses several in a row eventually; abandoning it during a normal losing run and
starting again is how people manage to lose money with strategies that would have worked.
